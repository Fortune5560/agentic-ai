Consent kit

A consent record for community mental-health work, where the participant's limits are part of the data: what may be written, in which language, for how long, and who may read it. The rules live in the code, so a record that breaks them cannot be saved.

  • Runs offline
  • No model in the record path
  • 24-case answer key
  • Sealed with AES-GCM

What this is: an architecture sketch, written in public, with its rules and tests exposed. What it is not: a validated clinical tool. No community health worker has reviewed it yet, every participant below is invented, and the Yorùbá statements are an unreviewed draft translation — the code marks them status: "draft" so that claim can be checked rather than trusted.

    4. The small local model

    The model never writes the record. Its only job is to read a messy note and propose the structured fields a health worker then confirms. That keeps the failure small: a wrong suggestion is visible and refusable, not baked in.

    It prints exact-match accuracy against the 20-item key, mean tokens per second on your hardware, the peak RAM drop while it works, and every wrong answer verbatim — including the ones that matter most, where the policy says refuse and the model says accept. The report is written to consent-lab/model/results/ so it can be published unchanged.

    5. What is missing

    • No review by health workers yet. The consent wording, the five scopes and the age rule are my drafts. The next step is twelve conversations with community health workers in Ibadan, not more code.
    • The Yorùbá is a draft. It is marked draft in the data and on this page. Publishing a translation as reviewed would be worse than publishing none.
    • No device storage yet. Records live in memory for this demo; the sealed blob is real, the place it would be written is not.
    • No key management. A passphrase typed by a health worker is not a key ceremony. That is a design problem I have not solved, and saying so is cheaper than pretending.
    • No clinical claim. Nothing here is a medical device, a diagnostic tool, or a substitute for supervision.
